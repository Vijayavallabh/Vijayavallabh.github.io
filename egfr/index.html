Work / Adaptyv EGFR Challenge 2026

A binder that switches on inside the tumour.

EGFR is a protein that sits on the surface of many cancer cells. It also sits on healthy skin and gut cells, so drugs that grab it cause real side effects. These twenty designed proteins grab EGFR only where a tumour turns its surroundings acidic, and let go again in normal blood.

The problem

A good target in the wrong places

EGFR drives growth in many cancers, including colorectal, head and neck, and lung tumours, which makes it one of the most validated targets in oncology. The approved antibodies against it, cetuximab and panitumumab, work. They also reach the EGFR on healthy skin and gut lining, and that causes the rash and gut problems that limit how much a patient can take.

The target is not the problem. Telling tumour tissue apart from healthy tissue is. A binder that could read its surroundings and act only inside a tumour would keep the benefit and drop much of the cost.

  • 3cancers where EGFR is a major drug target: colorectal, head and neck, lung
  • 2approved anti-EGFR antibodies, both limited by skin and gut side effects

The opening

Tumours are acidic. Use that.

Solid tumours run their metabolism fast and sloppily, and the tissue around them turns acidic as a result. Outside a tumour cell the pH sits near 6.5. Normal blood holds a steady 7.4. That gap of under one pH unit is the one physical signal that marks tumour tissue almost everywhere a solid tumour grows.

Read half a pH unit, and you can tell a tumour from the rest of the body.

A binder that senses that difference would switch on in the acidic margin and stay quiet in the bloodstream. The whole design turns on building a reliable pH sensor into a protein that is small, novel, and still binds hard.

The switch

One amino acid does the sensing

Of the twenty amino acids, histidine is the only one that changes its electrical charge right in the window between tumour and blood. In acid it picks up a small positive charge. Near neutral it gives that charge up. The crossover sits close to pH 6.3, which lands neatly between 6.5 and 7.4.

Put a positively charged histidine next to a negatively charged partner and the two snap together into a salt bridge, the same attraction that holds table salt in a crystal. Take the charge away and the bridge falls apart. One histidine, one on/off switch, tuned by the pH of the room. Drag the slider above and the whole binder follows that one charge.

Inverted · 2 designs

The binder carries the histidine itself

It bridges to a fixed acidic spot on EGFR, Asp460.

This is not a brand-new idea in nature, which is a good sign. An engineered anti-EGFR antibody called G532 already uses the same histidine trick, binds about 8 times more tightly at pH 6.5 than at 7.4, and works on both human and mouse EGFR. These twenty designs rebuild that chemistry from scratch in a single small chain, rather than grafting it onto an antibody.

Building

Drawing twenty proteins that did not exist

The proteins were built with three AI design tools, each doing one job. RFdiffusion shapes a backbone that can reach the EGFR surface and hold the switch in place. ProteinMPNN fills in the amino acids, with the switch residue pinned and no cysteines allowed, so the proteins are easier to make in a lab. AlphaFold2 then folds each sequence against EGFR and keeps only the ones that fold cleanly and dock where they should.

Every design is a single chain of 56 to 108 amino acids. None reuses a known protein: all twenty passed the challenge's own novelty check, which compares each one against the entire public protein-structure record.

The real molecule

This is design 23

The switch above is a cartoon. This is the real thing: the predicted fold of design 23, the 108-residue protein that tops the panel and anchors the bench test. It folds into a tight bundle of helices that holds the switch acid, Glu66, out on the face that meets EGFR.

Turn it with the mouse. The amino acid drawn as thick sticks is Glu66, the one that reaches across to EGFR's histidine and forms the pH-sensitive grip. Change that single residue and you get design 32, the broken twin from the bench test.

The proof

Showing the switch actually flips

Here is the catch. AlphaFold2 and the other folding tools say nothing about pH. They can tell you a binder docks, but not whether it docks harder in acid than in blood, which is the entire point. So the pH behaviour is checked separately, on the exact submitted sequence, by four methods that have to agree on the direction of the switch.

Acid/base reading 1pKa from structure Predicts which way the histidine tips and by how much.
Acid/base reading 2independent calculator A second, machine-learned estimate of the same thing, to catch disagreement.
Two-pH simulationthe decisive one The binder is simulated twice, once with the histidine charged and once neutral, measuring how much of the time the salt bridge stays formed. A bridge that holds in both states is useless, and those designs were cut.
Binding energyfrom the same runs Estimates how much stronger the grip is in acid than in blood.

The two-pH simulation is the one that matters. A good acid/base score only shows the switch points the right way. It cannot tell a real switch from a bridge that happens to stay glued together whether the histidine is charged or not, and that kind of bridge binds everywhere, tumour and blood alike. Running the two charge states separately is what exposes the difference.

Twelve of the twenty pass all four checks. The rest pass the simulation and the binding check. As a control, the same method correctly re-finds a known pH switch in a natural antibody-receptor pair, so it is not just rubber-stamping the designs.

Cross-reactivity

It has to work in mice too

A binder that only grabs human EGFR cannot be tested in animals, and no drug reaches patients without that step. So each design has to bind mouse EGFR as well. The switch spot was chosen with this in mind: His433 and Asp460 are identical in human and mouse, so the part that does the sensing is the same protein in both species.

Four independent checks agree the designs bind both. Every contact point on the epitope is shared between the two species, a matched human-versus-mouse re-fold shows no loss of grip, the surface shapes line up, and folding against mouse EGFR holds up.

Built in for the bench

A test the lab can run on day one

Two of the designs are the same protein, except one amino acid is changed to switch off the pH response. Design 23 should bind harder in acid. Design 32, the broken twin, should bind the same at both pH. Run the pair side by side and you read the mechanism directly: if 23 prefers acid and 32 does not, the designed salt bridge is what drives the switch. Test this pair first.

The limit

Why histidine, and nothing fancier

A stronger switch would be worth a lot, so several were tried and scored with a fair, chemistry-neutral set of tools: stacked aromatic rings, a buried charge pair, a hinged lid that opens with pH. Each failed for a concrete reason. The stacked rings are no stronger than the salt bridge and their stronger form is not novel. Buried charges cannot be predicted reliably enough to trust the flip point. The hinged lid would not build as a clean two-part fold.

Histidine is the only amino acid whose tipping point sits inside the tumour-to-blood window and can be predicted with confidence. A histidine salt bridge, forward or inverted, is the practical ceiling on this target. The panel sits at that ceiling rather than reaching past it on a claim that would not hold.

What this is, and isn't

The honest part

No one has yet shown in a lab that a designed single-chain binder flips on and off with pH through this mechanism. The evidence here is agreement across independent calculations, the two-pH simulations, and the built-in broken-twin control. It is not a measured binding curve.

That is exactly why the matched pair exists. It turns the whole claim into one clean experiment a lab can run early, before committing to the rest. The design is made to be checked, not just trusted.
