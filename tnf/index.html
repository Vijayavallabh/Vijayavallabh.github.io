A gripper that lets go.

These designed mini-proteins grab human TNF out of the bloodstream and release it in the acid inside the cell, so one copy can come back for another. One of the firmest human switches did the reverse against mouse TNF, which is why the switch was measured, one design at a time, in both species.

The target

An alarm worth switching off

TNF is a small protein that cells release to raise the alarm. In short bursts it runs a normal immune response. When it will not stand down, it drives rheumatoid arthritis, Crohn’s disease and psoriasis.

The best-selling drugs against it, adalimumab and infliximab, work by mopping TNF up, which is strong proof the target is a good one. Nearly all of those drugs are antibodies, large and all built the same way; the one exception, etanercept, is a piece of the natural receptor rather than a new shape. No small, newly designed protein had managed to stick to TNF at all. Its surface is smooth and water-loving, the kind of face that de-novo binders slide off.

So the task set by the challenge is pointed: draw a small protein that grabs this slippery target, releases it on cue, and does both in human and mouse. Everything below is computational. There is no lab result yet; the point of the work is to name the designs worth putting on a bench first.

  • 3diseases driven by TNF that stays on: rheumatoid arthritis, Crohn’s, psoriasis
  • 2top-selling anti-TNF antibody drugs, proof the target is right
What was built

A protein that never existed

Each binder was drawn from scratch rather than borrowed from nature. Three tools, one job each. RFdiffusion sketches a backbone shaped to reach the groove on TNF. ProteinMPNN picks the amino acids, with no cysteines, which makes the protein easier to make. AlphaFold2 then folds each sequence back against TNF and keeps only the ones that fold cleanly and land where they should.

Because TNF is flat and water-loving, most drawn backbones slide off, so that folding filter does most of the cutting. What survives is a single chain of about 80 amino acids, far smaller than an antibody, carrying no cysteines. Every design is genuinely new: all of them clear the challenge’s novelty gate, which checks each one against the whole known record of protein structures and needs a pass of at least three of four.

Why a switch at all

Letting go is the whole point

An ordinary binder catches one TNF and holds it forever, spent after a single grab. A binder that releases can ride into the cell, drop its cargo to be destroyed, and come back empty for the next one. That reuse is the reason to build a switch at all.

The signal

Acid is an address

The binder has no idea where it is in the body. It can only read the acidity of the water around it, the way a thermostat reads temperature. That one reading is enough. Blood is held at a steady pH 7.4, which counts as neutral. Inside the cell, the sorting bubble that breaks things down runs acidic, close to pH 6.0.

A drop of about one pH unit is a dependable “you are now inside the cell” signal.

So the job is narrow and physical: build a protein that grips at 7.4 and releases at 6.0. The whole design turns on reading half a pH unit and acting on it.

The mechanism

The switch works backward

Of the twenty amino acids, histidine is the only one that changes its charge right in the window between blood and endosome. Near 7.4 it is neutral. Near 6.0 it picks up a positive charge. The other nineteen sit still across this range.

Here is the part that runs against intuition. Each switch histidine is placed next to a lysine on TNF that is positive the whole time. At pH 7.4 the histidine is neutral and the binder grips. At pH 6.0 the histidine turns positive, and now two like charges sit together and shove apart, the way the same poles of two magnets refuse to meet. The push pries the grip open. Acid makes this binder let go, the opposite of the usual “more charge, more sticky”. The designs carry two or more of these histidines, each against its own conserved lysine, so the release does not rest on a single contact.

Cross-reactivity

It has to work in a mouse

No binder reaches patients without an animal test first, and that test comes before any human trial. So the same protein has to grab mouse TNF as well. The switch was placed with this in mind, on a patch that both species share: human and mouse TNF are 89–95% identical across the contact patch, and the target lysines are present in both.

That makes a strong case that the binder should still grab mouse TNF. But that is a look-alike argument about shape. Whether the pH switch still fires in a mouse is a separate question, and the honest answer is that a matching shape does not settle it. The next two sections are how that question got measured instead of assumed.

The measurement

Weighing a switch before it exists

Folding tools say nothing about pH. They tell you a binder docks, not whether it grips harder in blood than in acid, which is the entire point. So the switch is checked on its own, three ways that have to agree on direction, and then it is measured.

The measurement is a free-energy calculation. It charges the switch histidine from neutral to positive twice: once while the binder grips TNF, once while the binder floats free. Then it takes the difference of the two energy costs. If charging the histidine costs more while gripping than while free, the new charge is fighting the grip, so the binder releases in acid. That is reported as a positive number.

Taking the difference is the trick. It cancels the messy terms that both runs share and leaves only the part that depends on gripping. That is why the direction of the switch can be trusted even though the exact size cannot: the slow part of the simulation never fully settles, so size is left to the lab. The direction was read at 6 independent runs, then 12, then 24 where a close call needed it. That discipline mattered: it caught two designs whose switch runs the wrong way, binding tighter in acid, and both were cut.

The result

A strong human switch ran backward in mouse

Running that free-energy test against mouse TNF was the first time the switch was checked by energy in a second species, rather than argued from a shared patch: 18 designs, both species, 216 simulations. The result reordered the shortlist instead of confirming it. How strong a switch is in human turns out to say almost nothing about mouse.

Each line below runs from a design’s human result on the left to its mouse result on the right. Above the zero line, the binder releases in acid, the way it should; below, the switch has reversed. Five lines hold in the blue band on both sides. One line that starts high on the human side, des_4098, plunges straight through zero into red: a top-ranked human switcher that grips harder in acid against mouse TNF.

Five designs release in both human and mouse: des_6025, des_820, b4m2, des_4082a and des_732. Those are the safest first choices to put into an animal, and the submission leads with them. The lesson underneath is the useful part: the mouse number carried information the human number could not, so it was worth paying for one design at a time.

What this is, and isn’t

The honest part

This is computational evidence, and it is worth being exact about what it can and cannot carry.

  • trust itWhich way the switch goes. Checked three ways and then measured by free energy. Reliable for direction.
  • trust itWhich five designs carry the switch into mouse. From the energy test in both species.
  • hold lightlyThe mouse pose. It is a predicted shape for how the binder sits on mouse TNF, screened against the human design that was actually drawn. There is no lab structure, and no test proves a predicted pose is exactly right.
  • lab onlyHow big the switch is, and the final grip strength. The simulation will not settle the size, and binding strength on this target is close to a coin flip in software. These are bench numbers.

No one has yet shown a designed single-chain switch of this kind flipping on a bench. So this is the strongest evidence available before the lab, and its job is to name the few designs worth testing first, not to call the result in.

Sources

Tools and method

  • Design. RFdiffusion for backbones, ProteinMPNN for sequences, AlphaFold2 initial-guess for the binding filter, Boltz-2 and Chai-1 for a second opinion on the pose.
  • The switch, checked. Two-state molecular dynamics in OpenMM with a histidine-to-glutamine control, plus PROPKA and pKAI acid-base estimates and a GFN2-xTB charge calculation, all agreeing on direction.
  • The switch, measured. Alchemical free energy of charging the histidine, bound complex versus free binder, by thermodynamic integration in OpenMM, run to 24 independent seeds where needed, in both human and mouse.
  • Honesty. Everything is computational prediction and simulation. The full methods and per-design data live in the project repository, which opens to the public after the challenge closes.
