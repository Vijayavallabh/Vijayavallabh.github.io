A gripper that lets go.

I designed small proteins that grab TNF, the alarm signal behind a lot of inflammation, out of the blood and let it go once they carry it inside a cell, where it turns acidic. Letting go is the point: it means one protein can come back and grab another. The twist is that the design that did this best in people did the opposite in mice, so I measured the switch in both, one design at a time.

The target

An alarm worth switching off

TNF is a tiny protein your cells fire off to raise an alarm. A short burst is normal defense. When it will not switch off, it drives diseases like rheumatoid arthritis, Crohn’s and psoriasis.

The best-selling drugs against it work by soaking it up, which tells me the target is a good one. Almost all of them are antibodies: big molecules, all built the same way. (The one exception, etanercept, is a piece of the body’s own receptor, not a new shape.) I wanted to see whether something much smaller and brand new could do the same job. The hard part is that TNF is smooth and water-loving, the kind of surface small designed proteins usually just slide off.

Everything here is done on a computer. There is no lab result yet. The point is to pick the designs most worth testing on a bench, and to be honest about how sure I am of each one.

What was built

A protein that never existed

I didn’t copy anything from nature. I drew each protein from scratch with a few AI tools, each doing one job. One sketches a shape that can reach TNF. One fills in what the shape is made of. One checks that it actually folds up and lands in the right spot. Most shapes slide off TNF, so that last check throws almost everything away.

What is left is a single strand about 80 building blocks long, far smaller than an antibody, with none of the sticky parts (cysteines) that make proteins hard to manufacture. And every design is genuinely new: each one passes the contest’s originality check against every protein structure on record.

Why a switch at all

Letting go is the whole point

A normal grabber catches one TNF and never lets go, so it is used up after a single catch. A grabber that can release does something better: it carries TNF into the cell, drops it where the cell destroys it, and comes back empty for the next one. That reuse is the whole reason I built a switch.

The signal

Acid is an address

My protein has no idea where it is in the body. The one thing it can sense is how acidic the water around it is, the way a thermostat senses temperature. That turns out to be enough. Blood is mild, a steady pH 7.4. The little pocket inside a cell that breaks things down is more acidic, close to pH 6.0.

That small change is a reliable sign that I have gone from the blood into a cell.

So the job is simple to state: hold on at 7.4, let go at 6.0. The whole design comes down to reading half a step of acidity and acting on it.

The mechanism

The switch works backward

Proteins are built from twenty kinds of building block. Exactly one of them, histidine, flips from neutral to positively charged right in the gap between blood and cell. Near 7.4 it is neutral. Near 6.0 it turns positive. The other nineteen do not budge across this range.

Here is the part that runs against intuition. I put one of these switches right next to a spot on TNF (a lysine) that is positively charged the whole time. At pH 7.4 the switch is neutral and my protein grips. At pH 6.0 the switch turns positive too, and now two positives sit side by side and shove apart, the way two magnets refuse to meet if you flip one around. That push pops the grip open. Acid makes my protein let go, the opposite of the usual “more charge, more sticky”. I use two or more of these switches, each against its own positive spot, so the release never hangs on a single contact.

Cross-reactivity

It has to work in a mouse

No drug gets near people without being tested in animals first. So my protein also has to grab mouse TNF. I picked the switch spot with that in mind. Across the patch my protein grabs, human and mouse TNF are almost the same, 89–95% identical, and the positive spots it pushes against are there in both.

That is a good reason to expect it to grab mouse TNF too. But matching shape only tells me it should grab. Whether the switch still fires in a mouse is a separate question, and looking similar does not answer it. The next two sections are how I measured that instead of assuming it.

The measurement

Weighing a switch before it exists

The folding tools tell me a protein sticks to TNF, but nothing about acidity, not whether it sticks harder in blood than in acid, which is the whole point. So I check the switch on its own. Three quick methods have to agree on which way it leans, and then I measure it properly.

The measurement is the interesting part. I charge up that one switch spot from neutral to positive twice: once while my protein is gripping TNF, and once while it is floating free on its own. Then I subtract. If charging it up costs more energy while gripping, the new charge is fighting the grip, so my protein lets go in acid. I report that as a positive number.

Subtracting is what makes it trustworthy. The messy stuff that is the same in both cases cancels out, leaving only the part that depends on gripping. That is why I trust which way the switch goes even when I cannot trust exactly how strong it is: the slow part of the simulation never fully settles, so I leave the size to the lab. I read the direction over 6 runs, then 12, then 24 where it was a close call. That care paid off: it caught two designs whose switch ran the wrong way, gripping tighter in acid, and I cut both.

Weigh a backpack by standing on a scale with it, then without it, and subtract.

The result

A strong human switch ran backward in mouse

I had argued before that these should work in mice because the shapes match. This was the first time I actually measured the switch, by energy rather than by matching shapes, against mouse TNF: 18 designs, both species, 216 runs. The answer reshuffled my shortlist instead of just confirming it. How well a switch works in human barely predicts how it does in mouse.

Each line below runs from a design’s human result on the left to its mouse result on the right. Above the line in the middle, my protein lets go in acid, the way I want; below it, the switch has flipped. Five lines stay up in the blue on both sides. One that starts high on the human side, des_4098, drops straight through into the red: a design that is great in people but grips harder in acid against mouse TNF.

releases in acid (positive) runs backward (negative) flat / borderline left = human · right = mouse

Five designs let go in both human and mouse: des_6025, des_820, b4m2, des_4082a and des_732. Those are my safest first picks to try in an animal, and I put them at the front of what I am submitting. The real lesson is underneath: the mouse number told me something the human number could not, so it was worth paying for each one.

What this is, and isn’t

The honest part

This is all computer work, so let me be straight about what it can and cannot tell you.

  • trust itWhich way each switch leans. I checked it three ways and then measured it. I trust this.
  • trust itWhich five designs keep the switch working in mouse. From the energy test in both species.
  • hold lightlyHow my protein sits on mouse TNF. That is a predicted shape, lined up against the human design I actually drew. There is no lab structure, and nothing proves a predicted shape is exactly right.
  • lab onlyHow strong the switch is, and how tightly it finally grips. The simulation will not pin the size down, and grip strength is close to a guess in software. Those are bench numbers.

Nobody has shown a small designed switch like this flipping in a real experiment yet. So this is the best evidence I can give before the lab, and its job is to point at the few designs worth testing first, not to call the result.

Sources

Tools and method

  • Design. RFdiffusion for backbones, ProteinMPNN for sequences, AlphaFold2 initial-guess for the binding filter, Boltz-2 and Chai-1 for a second opinion on the pose.
  • The switch, checked. Two-state molecular dynamics in OpenMM with a histidine-to-glutamine control, plus PROPKA and pKAI acid-base estimates and a GFN2-xTB charge calculation, all agreeing on direction.
  • The switch, measured. Alchemical free energy of charging the histidine, bound complex versus free binder, by thermodynamic integration in OpenMM, run to 24 independent seeds where needed, in both human and mouse.
  • Honesty. Everything is computational prediction and simulation. The full methods and per-design data live in the project repository, which opens to the public after the challenge closes.
