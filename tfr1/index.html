Built to let go.

Nearly every cell in your body carries a tiny grabber on its surface that pulls iron inside. It is called TfR1. Because it ferries cargo from the bloodstream into the cell, drug makers treat it as a side door: hook a medicine onto something TfR1 carries, and you can reach places that are otherwise sealed off, the brain most of all. The hard part is the timing. A small protein that latches onto TfR1 is only useful if it holds tight out in the blood, where the acidity is mild (pH 7.4), and then lets go the instant the cell swallows it into an acid pocket inside (pH 6.0). Hold on too long and the cell hauls the whole bundle off to be destroyed, instead of handing it back out to work again. I designed twenty of these small proteins from scratch, each built around that let‑go‑on‑cue switch, and each meant to work on the human and the mouse versions of TfR1.

All of this is done on a computer. Nothing has been tested at a lab bench yet, so the whole project hinges on one honest problem: the main way I check my work quietly breaks on this particular target. The check is meant to tell me where on the receptor a design lands. To trust it, I first ran it on a protein that is already known, from a real solved structure, to grab the right spot. On the human receptor, that known‑good protein scored worse than shuffled, meaningless sequences. If the test ranks a real binder below gibberish, it cannot tell me where anything lands. It can still tell me that a design grabs the receptor better than random, and nothing finer than that. So I rebuilt the whole selection on the parts that did still work: the mouse version of the receptor, where the same check does tell real from nonsense; the known‑good protein as a yardstick; and a second test that forces each design onto the right spot and measures how well it actually sits there. That combination caught two of my own designs faking it, scoring high while never really touching the target.

The target

A doorway into the cell

TfR1 (doctors also call it CD71) sits on the surface of almost every growing cell and pulls iron inside. It works like a tiny ferry: it grabs its cargo out in the blood, folds inward carrying the cargo in a little bubble, drops the cargo once that bubble turns acidic, and then rides back out empty to do it all again. That round trip is why drug developers love it. Attach your medicine to something that hitches onto TfR1, and you can reach places that are otherwise walled off from the bloodstream, the brain most of all.

The catch is that acid step. A protein that clings on too tightly never gets dropped off; it stays stuck to the ferry, rides it into the cell's garbage system, and both get shredded. So a useful design has to do the opposite of what protein binders are usually built for. Most are built to hold on as hard as they can. This one has to hold on in the blood and then deliberately let go the moment its surroundings turn acidic, as the acidity climbs from mild (pH 7.4) to clearly acidic (pH 6.0).

The contest sets the bar. It asks for a protein invented from scratch, not copied from anything in nature or in a database, that does three things in order of importance. First, it has to hold on in the blood and let go in the acid. Second, it should work on both the human and the mouse receptor, so it can be tried in animals before people. Third, it has to grab the part of the receptor that actually matters, the bit that sticks out and does the ferrying. Being genuinely new is non‑negotiable. Everything I report is a computer's best guess; the point of all of it is to pick the handful of designs most worth actually making and testing.

The mechanism

The one building block that feels the acid

Proteins are built from a kit of twenty small parts called amino acids. The jump from blood to acid pocket is a small change, and out of all twenty parts only one noticeably reacts to it: histidine. In the acid it grabs a stray positive charge and turns slightly positive; back in the blood it falls quiet and neutral. Its flip happens right in the gap between the two, which makes histidine the only built‑in acid sensor I have to work with. So the switch is simply a few histidines placed exactly where the designed protein touches the receptor. In the blood they sit quiet and the protein holds on. In the acid they light up, turn positive, and shove the two surfaces apart until the protein falls off.

Getting the direction right is the hard part, and it is easy to get backwards. Drop a histidine in the wrong place and its charge pulls the two surfaces together in the acid instead of pushing them apart, which is exactly the opposite of what I want. So I leaned on a tool that predicts, for each histidine, the precise acidity at which it flips. I kept only designs with at least two histidines that flip inside the narrow range between blood and acid (about pH 5.5 to 7.4), then compared each design in both situations, stuck on in the blood and charged up in the acid, to make sure the push really ran the right way.

How I looked

Drawing proteins for a flat, slippery patch

The part of TfR1 I need to grab is a problem in itself: it is flat and smooth, with no deep pocket or groove to get a grip on. Most of the popular design methods work by dropping a little protein coil into a groove, and here there simply is no groove. The approach that worked builds the protein's shape first, aimed at the small scrap of that flat surface that human and mouse have in common. A second program then chooses the sequence of building blocks to fit each shape. I set it to avoid one sticky building block (cysteine) that tends to make proteins clump and hard to manufacture. For the designs that had to work on both species, I used a version that writes a single sequence to fit the human and mouse surfaces at once.

To check a design without a lab, I fed each one, paired with the real receptor, to a program that predicts how two proteins fold together and fit, working only from their sequences and nothing it has seen before. This is the same kind of program the contest uses to score entries. Every time I ran it, I slipped in two reference points: a protein already proven to grab the right spot (from a real solved structure), and shuffled, meaningless sequences standing in as fakes. A design had to beat the fakes to count at all. Then, for the reason in the next section, I confirmed the survivors a second way, with a test that forces the protein onto the correct patch and measures how snugly it actually sits.

Where the measurement broke

The ruler that couldn't see

To judge a design you want to know two things: does it grab the receptor at all, and does it grab the right spot. For that second question I used the standard measure, which is roughly how much of the correct patch the protein ends up covering. Then I checked that measure against my reference points. On the human receptor, the shuffled nonsense sequences covered the patch just as well as, or better than, the protein that is actually known to bind it.

So on the human receptor, that spot‑check is blind. The surface is so flat and featureless that the program slides every protein across it the same way, real or nonsense, and cannot tell where any of them truly land. The one honest thing this kind of test can say is that a design grabs the receptor better than random. It cannot say the design grabs the right spot. A measure that rejects the very protein I know to be correct is measuring noise, so I threw it out.

Two things still worked. The mouse version of the same surface is just bumpy enough that the test does tell the real binder apart from the fakes. And the second test, the one that forces a protein onto the correct patch and scores how well it sits, holds up against my reference points too. I rebuilt the whole selection on those two. Then I ran every one of the twenty designs again, side by side with the reference points, and had a fresh, skeptical reviewer try to tear the result apart. The mouse side caught two designs that scored high but never really landed on the patch. I swapped both out. Catching that is the result I am proudest of here.

What this is, and isn’t

The honest part

This is all computer work, so here is what it can and cannot honestly tell you.

  • trust itEach design grabs the receptor better than random, and holds together as a real, foldable protein. Every design beat the shuffled fakes, and the forcing test confirms the real ones can sit on the correct patch. I trust this much.
  • hold lightlyHow well it works on mouse, and the exact way it sits. The mouse signal is real but noisy, and the predicted pose is a guess, not a solved structure. I compare designs on equal terms and pick for variety, rather than reading too much into any single number.
  • lab onlyWhether a design truly lands on the human patch, and how strong the let‑go switch really is. The flat surface makes the first impossible to measure on a computer, and the switch's exact strength is finer than any prediction tool can call. Only a real lab test settles either one.

This limit is the same no matter which set I had sent; it comes from the receptor itself, not from any one design. The job of these twenty is to point a lab at a varied, promising group to try, each with an honest label on how sure I am of it.

The set

Twenty, built to release

All twenty carry a working let‑go switch (at least two acid‑sensing histidines that flip in the right range), none contain the sticky building block that hurts manufacturing, and all are genuinely new. Most come from the shape‑first design runs, plus a couple built to fit human and mouse at once. The “binds” column says where the evidence holds up: human only, or human and mouse. They are listed best first, with the ten most likely to be chosen for the lab at the top. Tools and method

  • How the proteins were designed. The shapes come first, aimed at the small patch human and mouse share; a program then chooses a sequence of building blocks to fit each shape. For the record: backbones from Genie3, conditioned on the conserved apical sub‑site, with RFdiffusion4/Proteina for fold diversity; sequences from a solubility‑tuned, cysteine‑free ProteinMPNN; the both‑species designs from a multi‑state tied ProteinMPNN written against the human and mouse apical surfaces together. Natural amino acids, single chain, new structures.
  • How I checked binding. Each design was folded together with the target by a structure predictor given no related examples (ESMFold2 + Protenix), with a known binder and shuffled sequences as controls on every run. On the human surface the where‑does‑it‑land measure fails its own known‑good control, so there I read binding only as “beats the shuffled fakes”; landing on the right patch is confirmed separately by a docking run that holds the protein on the patch (pocket‑restrained Boltz‑2) against the same controls, and human/mouse cross‑reactivity is read on the mouse surface, where the measure still works.
  • How I checked the switch. A tool that predicts each histidine's flip point kept only designs with at least two histidines flipping in the right acidity range, plus a two‑state check that the charge loosens the grip in acid rather than tightening it.
  • How I kept myself honest. I threw out the human where‑does‑it‑land measure once it failed its own controls, re‑folded all twenty with controls in one run, and had a cold, adversarial review attack the result; it caught two designs that scored high on the mouse side without touching the patch, and I replaced both. Everything here is a computer prediction. The full method and per‑design data live in the project repository, public after the challenge closes.
