The one buried letter.

A cancer cell and a healthy cell can look almost the same from the outside. Both chop up their own proteins and hang little ten‑letter fragments on their surface, clipped into a display stand that the immune system reads like a flag. In one of the most common cancer mutations, a protein called KRAS has a single building block swapped, glycine for aspartate, a change written as G12D. A cell carrying it flies a flag that is almost identical to the one on a healthy cell. The two read as ten letters, and they differ at exactly one position. I designed twenty proteins from scratch to grab the cancer flag and leave the healthy one alone.

The hard part is where that one letter sits. It does not stick up on the exposed face of the flag, where a protein could reach out and touch it. It points down into the groove of the display stand, almost fully buried, where it quietly pairs with a part of the stand itself. So a binder cannot read the letter that matters by touching it. It has to hold the parts of the flag that do stick out and sense the hidden letter from a short distance, through the faint charge it carries and the way it makes the flag settle. All of this runs on a computer; nothing has reached a lab bench yet. And the project turns on one honest fact: the usual way to check these designs is blind to that single buried letter. Telling the cancer flag from the healthy one is a question only the bench can answer. What the computer can do is line the twenty up by every other clue and push the most promising few to the front.

The target

A flag that differs by one letter

The cancer flag is a ten‑letter fragment of the mutated KRAS protein. In the shorthand biologists use it reads VVVGADGVGK. The healthy version reads VVVGAGGVGK. Line the two up and only the sixth letter changes: a D (aspartate) on the cancer flag, a G (glycine) on the healthy one. Everything else is identical. The display stand holding the flag is called HLA, and the exact version here, A*11:01, is one of the most common in East and South Asia, which is part of why this marker is worth chasing.

That sixth letter is the whole problem. On the solved crystal structure it does not sit up on the open face of the flag. It points down into the groove of the stand, with only about a tenth of it exposed, and it pairs with a positive spot on the stand itself (Arg114). So the feature that makes the cancer flag different is tucked underneath, pressed against the stand, out of reach. Even the immune system's own receptors, the ones evolved over a lifetime to read these flags, struggle to tell this pair apart.

Why bother, then. Because if a protein can grab the cancer flag and ignore the healthy one, it can mark cancer cells for the immune system while leaving the rest of the body alone. The contest asks for exactly that: a protein invented from scratch, not copied from anything in nature or a database, that grabs the G12D flag on HLA‑A*11:01 and not the wild‑type flag, not other unrelated flags, and not the empty stand. Being genuinely new is required. Everything I report is a computer's best guess, and the point of it is to choose the handful most worth making and testing for real.

The mechanism

Reading a letter you cannot touch

Because the sixth letter is buried, a binder cannot read it the direct way, by reaching in and feeling its shape. Some binders for other mutations get to do that, where the changed letter points up into the open and the protein builds a little pocket around it. Here that route is closed. The letter faces the wrong way.

Two things still leak out of the groove. The aspartate on the cancer flag carries a small negative charge and the glycine on the healthy flag carries none, so the two flags sit in a slightly different electrical field, and that difference is still faintly there a short distance away, up where a binder can be. And the two flags do not settle into the groove the same way; the cancer flag's loop is a little more restless when nothing is holding it. A design that works grips the parts of the flag that stick up, at positions 4, 5, 7 and 8, and is shaped so it slightly prefers the field and the settling of the cancer flag. It reads the hidden letter the way you feel a coin through a thin cloth, without ever seeing it.

This is also why there is an obvious trap to avoid. A protein that grabbed the broad flat sides of the display stand, instead of the flag sitting in it, would stick to almost every cell in the body, because every cell flies some flag on the very same stand. So I aimed the designs at the flag and the groove right around it, and steered them away from the stand's general surface. A binder that holds the platform rather than the flag is worse than useless, and a good chunk of the checking below exists to catch that.

How I looked

Two design engines, one honest filter

I built the twenty designs with two separate programs that invent protein shapes from nothing, one called BoltzGen and one called Genie3, so that a blind spot in one engine would not sink the whole set. A second program then wrote the sequence of building blocks for each shape, set to skip a sticky building block (cysteine) that makes proteins hard to manufacture. None of the designs is copied from a known binder. The result is twenty proteins, fourteen small single‑chain ones and six built in the shape of a nanobody, a compact antibody fragment.

To test a design without a lab, I folded it together with the whole four‑part target at once, the flag, the display stand, a small helper piece, and the design itself, and looked at how the fold sits. For the main ranking I used a deliberately strict rule: reward a design for rejecting what the cancer and healthy flags have in common, the shared letters, the stand, the empty stand, rather than for raw stickiness. Grabbing hard is easy; grabbing selectively is the whole game, so the ranking is built around the weakest part of each design's case, not the strongest.

Then I added one test that can actually feel the buried letter. It is a physics calculation that swaps the sixth letter from D to G on the computer, on the same pose, and measures how much the design minds the swap. A design that truly prefers the cancer flag should mind a lot. This calculation is slow and only trustworthy when the pose is realistic, with that sixth letter still buried against Arg114 where it belongs, so I used it as a filter on a shortlist, never as the main ranking. Where the measurement stops

The score that can't see it

Here is the honest limit, and it sits at the centre of the whole project. The folding program that ranks everything is blind to the single buried letter. I checked this directly: when I fold the cancer flag and the healthy flag with the same design, the score barely moves, because the only thing that changed is tucked out of sight. So the folding score can tell me a design grabs the flag. It cannot tell me the design prefers the cancer flag over the healthy one.

That preference is a bench measurement. I never dress up a computer number as if it settled it. The physics filter can feel the buried letter, but only as a prior, and only as reliably as the one predicted pose it starts from. A pose is a guess. So I did not trust any single pose. I scored each promising design across five independent predicted poses and watched whether it kept preferring the cancer flag.

That gave two honest groups. Two designs preferred the cancer flag on all five poses and never flipped; I call those pose‑robust, and they lead the set. Three more preferred it on four of five, with one pose going the other way; I call those net‑leaning and I label them as such. The five‑pose check earned its keep. It caught one design that looked like the strongest in the whole campaign on a single lucky pose and fell to a coin‑flip across the five, so I dropped it. It also rescued one I had thrown out too early. Catching myself in both directions is the result I am most sure of here.

What this is, and isn’t

The honest part

This is all computer work, so here is what it can and cannot honestly tell you.

  • trust itEach design folds as a real, stable protein and grabs the flag better than random. The folding scores are high and the designs hold together. I trust this much.
  • hold lightlyThe exact pose, and the physics preference for the cancer flag. The preference comes from a calculation on a guessed pose. Two designs hold it on all five poses; three hold it on four of five. I read these as priors and pick for variety, not as proof.
  • lab onlyWhether a design truly prefers the cancer flag over the healthy one, and how strongly. The buried letter makes this impossible to settle on a computer. A lab test that stains cells with both flags is the only thing that decides it.

A passive binder also has a ceiling it cannot beat: even in the best case, the gap in how tightly it holds the two flags is set by that one buried letter, and for a single small protein that gap is modest, likely in the low tens of fold. The job of these twenty is to point a lab at a varied, promising group to try, each with an honest label on how sure I am.

The set

Twenty, ranked by evidence

All twenty come from the two design engines, run from scratch, and all are genuinely new. They are listed best first, with the ten most likely to be sent to the lab at the top. The “evidence” column says how strong the selectivity case is: pose‑robust for the two that prefer the cancer flag on all five poses, net‑leaning for the three that prefer it on four of five, and co‑fold for the well‑folded designs ranked on the strict shared‑rejection rule without a physics confirmation. The whole set passed the contest's automatic checks, 20 out of 20, with nothing flagged as too similar to anything known.

Sources

Tools and method

  • How the proteins were designed. Two independent engines invent the shapes from scratch, aimed at the flag and the groove around it: BoltzGen (protein and nanobody) for twelve of the designs, and Genie3, a diffusion model, for the other eight. A solubility‑tuned, cysteine‑free sequence designer (SolubleMPNN) then writes the building blocks for each shape. Natural amino acids, single chain or nanobody, new structures, no seeding from a known binder.
  • How I checked binding and selectivity. Each design was folded together with the full four‑part complex by a structure predictor (Boltz‑2), five times over six situations: the cancer flag, the healthy flag, a shuffled flag, two unrelated flags, and the empty stand. The ranking rewards the weakest of those gaps, so a design has to reject what the targets share, not just hold the cancer flag. The buried cancer‑versus‑healthy letter is left to the physics filter and the bench, never scored by the folding program, which cannot see it.
  • How I read the buried letter. An open‑source physics calculation (an ensemble MM‑GBSA free‑energy estimate in OpenMM) swaps the sixth letter from D to G on the matched pose and measures the change, run only on poses that keep that letter buried against Arg114. Each candidate was scored across five independent poses, and only designs that keep preferring the cancer flag are credited, two on all five poses and three on four of five.
  • How I kept myself honest. I set the selectivity bar before seeing the results, treated every folding number as a binder‑grabs‑the‑flag signal and never as proof of cancer‑versus‑healthy, and dropped a design that looked strongest on one lucky pose once five poses showed it was a coin‑flip. Everything here is a computer prediction. The full method and per‑design data live in the project repository, public after the challenge closes.
