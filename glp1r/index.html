A key that has to turn.

GLP‑1R is the receptor behind the new wave of weight‑loss and diabetes drugs — Ozempic, Wegovy and Mounjaro all work by switching it on. I tried to design a brand‑new protein, from scratch, that does the same: not just stick to the receptor, but turn it on. That last word is the whole problem. Making something that binds is doable. Knowing whether it switches the receptor on, before any lab test, turns out to be something no computer can tell you — and I watched my own best test for it get fooled by random sequences. So I did the honest thing: I stopped guessing activation, picked a wide, diverse set of strong binders, and left the verdict to the one judge that can give it.

The target

An on‑switch worth copying

GLP‑1R sits on cells in your gut, pancreas and brain. When the natural hormone GLP‑1 docks into it, it switches on a signal that helps control blood sugar and appetite. The best‑selling drugs of the last few years — semaglutide, tirzepatide — are peptides that do exactly this.

Every one of those drugs is a modified copy of the natural hormone. I wanted to see whether something genuinely new — not copied from GLP‑1 or any of its relatives — could switch the same receptor on. That rules out the easy route of starting from the hormone and tweaking it; the contest requires the design to be invented from scratch.

Everything here is done on a computer. There is no lab result yet. The job is to pick the designs most worth making on a bench, and to be honest about how sure I am of each one.

The hard part

Binding is the easy half

A protein that sticks to the outside of this receptor is, by default, a blocker — it gets in the way without turning anything on. Activation is a mechanical act: the front end of the ligand has to reach down into a pocket inside the seven‑helix bundle (the vestibule) and shove one inner helix, TM6, outward. That motion is what flips the switch on the inside of the cell.

That is a tall order for a small, made‑from‑scratch protein. When others have designed new binders to receptors of this family, the ones that worked came out as blockers, not activators. No standalone designed protein has been reported to switch on a receptor of this class at all. So this is a frontier bet, and I treated it like one: not a single confident winner, but a spread of credible attempts.

How I looked

Drawing proteins that reach the pocket

I didn’t copy anything from nature. Each design was drawn from scratch by several different AI generators — on purpose, so they don’t all share the same blind spot. Then every design was folded against the real receptor by two independent structure predictors (Boltz‑2 and Chai). A design only counted as reaching the pocket if both predictors agreed, across several random seeds — a guard against one model fooling itself. Every metric was calibrated against real controls: the natural agonists GLP‑1 and exendin‑4, and the known blocker exendin(9‑39).

The honest centre of this project

When my own test lied to me

I built a score for the one thing that matters — whether a design reaches deep enough to work the activation switch — and then I generated designs aimed straight at it. The fraction that engaged the switch jumped from 3% to 24%. For an afternoon it looked like a breakthrough.

Then I ran the same score on the controls I should never skip: scrambled, meaningless versions of GLP‑1, and the known blocker. They passed it just as well as the real agonists. The “24%” was an artifact of how I had set up the search, not a signal of activation. The score could not tell a switch‑flipper from random junk.

So I deleted it. For this receptor, no in‑silico score I have — or that exists — separates an activator from a blocker. I rebuilt the whole selection on what I can measure honestly, and let a cold outside review tear down the version that leaned on the fake signal. That reversal is the most important result in this project.

What this is, and isn’t

The honest part

This is all computer work, so let me be straight about what it can and cannot tell you.

  • trust itWhich designs bind and fold. Two independent predictors agree they reach the receptor’s vestibule and that the protein folds up cleanly. I trust the direction of this.
  • hold lightlyExactly how each one sits on the receptor. That is a predicted pose, not a solved structure, and the interface scores rise with length, so I compare like with like and don’t over‑read them.
  • lab onlyWhether any of them actually switches the receptor on. This is the whole prize, and nothing I can run decides it — the controls prove that. Only the cell‑based activation assay can call it.

Nobody has shown a small designed protein switching on a receptor of this class in a real experiment. So this is the best evidence I can give before the lab, and its job is to point at a diverse set worth testing — not to claim any of them is an agonist.

The set

Twenty shots, not one bet

Since I can’t pick the activator, I maximise the chance that at least one of a diverse set turns out to work. The twenty are spread across seven independent generators, capped so no single method dominates, all free of the sticky cysteines that make proteins hard to make, all genuinely new. They are ordered best‑first by measured binding, with the ten most likely to be tested at the top.

Tools and method

  • Design (de novo). Seven independent generators so biases don’t line up: BoltzGen, RFD4‑Proteina, RFdiffusion (with a structure‑conditioned motif run and extracellular‑domain scaffolds), DiffPepBuilder, PepGLAD, and a protein language model (PepMLM). Natural amino acids only, single chain, 10–250 residues, no meaningful overlap with GLP‑1, exendin‑4, glucagon or GIP.
  • The binding check. Each bare design co‑folded against wild‑type GLP‑1R (residues 24–432) with two predictors, Boltz‑2 and Chai, scored on interface confidence (ipSAE, ipTM), fold confidence (ESMFold2 pLDDT) and vestibule reach, with a two‑predictor agreement gate and native agonist / antagonist controls.
  • The honesty. The agonism signal was discarded after it failed its own scrambled and antagonist controls; a cold adversarial review was used to kill the version that relied on it. Everything is computational prediction. The full method and per‑design data live in the project repository, which opens to the public after the challenge closes.
