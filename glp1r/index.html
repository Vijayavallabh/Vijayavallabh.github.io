A key that has to turn.

GLP‑1R is the receptor that drugs like Ozempic and Wegovy act on: they work by switching it on. I wanted to design a brand‑new protein, from scratch, that does the same job. Making a protein that sticks to the receptor is doable. Telling whether it actually switches the receptor on, before anyone tests it in a dish, is the hard part, and for this receptor no computer can do it. I even watched my own best test for switching get fooled by scrambled sequences. So I stopped guessing which design works, picked a wide set of strong binders instead, and left that call to a lab.

The target

An on‑switch worth copying

GLP‑1R sits on cells in your gut and pancreas. When the natural hormone GLP‑1 docks into it, the receptor switches on a signal that helps control blood sugar and appetite. The drugs people know, semaglutide and tirzepatide, are peptides that do the same thing.

Every one of those drugs is a modified copy of the natural hormone. I wanted to see whether something new, not copied from GLP‑1 or any of its relatives, could switch the same receptor on. The contest requires exactly that: the design has to be invented from scratch, not tweaked from a hormone that already works.

Everything here was done on a computer. There is no lab result yet. The job is to pick the designs most worth making at a bench, and to say honestly how sure I am of each one.

The hard part

Binding is the easy half

A protein that sticks to the outside of this receptor is a blocker by default: it gets in the way without turning anything on. Switching the receptor on is a physical act. The front of the peptide has to reach down into a pocket inside the seven‑helix bundle (biologists call it the vestibule) and shove one helix, TM6, outward. That shove is what flips the switch on the inside of the cell.

That is a tall order for a small protein built from scratch. When other groups designed new binders to receptors in this family, the ones that worked came out as blockers rather than switches. No standalone designed protein has been reported to switch on a receptor of this class at all. So I treated it as a long shot and spread my bets across many credible attempts instead of backing just one.

How I looked

Drawing proteins that reach the pocket

I didn’t copy anything from nature. Several different AI generators drew each design from scratch, and I used more than one on purpose, so they would not all share the same blind spot. Then two independent structure predictors (Boltz‑2 and Chai) folded every design against the real receptor. A design only counted as reaching the pocket if both predictors agreed, across several random starting points; that is a guard against any one model fooling itself. I checked every score against known controls: the natural switches GLP‑1 and exendin‑4, and the known blocker exendin(9‑39).

Where I nearly fooled myself

When my own test lied to me

I built a score for the thing that actually matters: whether a design reaches deep enough to work the switch. Then I generated designs aimed straight at that score. The fraction that engaged the switch jumped from 3% to 24%. For an afternoon it looked like a breakthrough.

Then I ran the same score on the controls I should never skip: scrambled, meaningless versions of GLP‑1, and the known blocker. They passed just as well as the real switches. The “24%” was an artifact of how I had set up the search, not a sign of switching. The score could not tell a real switch from random junk.

So I deleted it. For this receptor, no computer score I have, or that exists, separates a switch from a blocker. I rebuilt the selection on what I can measure honestly: how well a design binds and folds. I also had a cold outside reviewer try to tear down the version that had leaned on the fake signal, and it did not survive. Catching that is the result I care about most here.

What this is, and isn’t

The honest part

This is all computer work, so let me be straight about what it can and cannot tell you.

  • trust itWhich designs bind and fold. Two independent predictors agree they reach the receptor’s vestibule and that the protein folds up cleanly. I trust the direction of this.
  • hold lightlyExactly how each one sits on the receptor. That is a predicted pose, not a solved structure, and the interface scores rise with length, so I compare like with like and don’t over‑read them.
  • lab onlyWhether any of them actually switches the receptor on. This is the real goal, and nothing I can run decides it. The controls prove that. Only a test in living cells can call it.

Nobody has shown a small designed protein switching on a receptor of this class in a real experiment. This is the best evidence I can give before the lab. Its job is to point at a varied set worth testing, and it makes no claim that any one design is a switch.

The set

Twenty shots, not one bet

Since I can’t pick the switch, I try to raise the odds that at least one design in a varied set turns out to work. The twenty come from seven different generators, with a cap so no single method dominates. None of them contain cysteine, the sticky amino acid that makes a protein hard to manufacture, and all are new. They run best‑first by measured binding, with the ten most likely to be tested at the top.

“Both folds” means both predictors agree the design reaches the pocket across repeated runs. “One fold” means only one does. “ECD site” means it binds the receptor’s outer domain, a different spot I kept as a hedge. None of these are lab measurements; every value is a prediction. And reaching the pocket is only a requirement for switching the receptor on, not proof that it happens.

Sources

Tools and method

  • Design (de novo). Seven independent generators, so their biases don’t line up: BoltzGen, RFD4‑Proteina, RFdiffusion (with a structure‑conditioned motif run and extracellular‑domain scaffolds), DiffPepBuilder, PepGLAD, and a protein language model (PepMLM). Natural amino acids only, single chain, 10 to 250 residues, no meaningful overlap with GLP‑1, exendin‑4, glucagon or GIP.
  • The binding check. Each bare design was co‑folded against wild‑type GLP‑1R (residues 24 to 432) with two predictors, Boltz‑2 and Chai, scored on interface confidence (ipSAE, ipTM), fold confidence (ESMFold2 pLDDT) and vestibule reach, with a two‑predictor agreement gate and native agonist and antagonist controls.
  • The honesty. I discarded the agonism signal after it failed its own scrambled and antagonist controls, and a cold adversarial review killed the version that had relied on it. Everything here is computational prediction. The full method and per‑design data live in the project repository, which opens to the public after the challenge closes.
